Introduction

The program area provides a variety of programs and services to assist unemployed people while addressing emerging labour market conditions throughout Manitoba. To meet the unique needs identified within communities, the program area delivers a number of these programs through contracts with service providers (organizations who deliver service on behalf of Manitoba).

The document provides a working definition of the indicated subject area. This defines the subject and indicates its importance to the program area activities. The service provider responsibilities references what the service provider is required to do in regard to the subject area. Caseworker responsibilities highlight requirements of the caseworker in the assessment or contractual process. The reference section, where applicable, indicates when there are more extensive procedures containing additional information about the subject.

The service provider/organization can be a private business, federal/provincial crown corporation, not-for-profit organization, employer/sector/employee association, public health and educational institution, municipal government or band/tribal council. For additional information regarding eligible service providers, please refer to the applicable program policy. They must operate the project in accordance with Manitoba Employment Standards regulations. An electronic copy of the Manitoba Employment Standards Code is available online: .

Description

This document provides program area caseworkers with the information required to assess, negotiate, document and deliver various contracted service provider agreements. The information provided is intended to be a process guide. Exceptional circumstances are to be discussed with the program area manager and the program area director when appropriate.

The assessment process is important to ensure that the proposal meets the program area’s standards and that it is realistic and feasible given the time available for the work, the level of skills required and the budget available.

Risk assessment

Working definition: conducting a risk assessment allows the caseworker to review the service provider, their administrative abilities, past experiences, the complexity of the proposal, the participant group, activities and budget. It assists in the assessment of the proposed project and determines the risks associated with contracting with the service provider. Completion of the risk assessment allows the caseworker to determine appropriate on-site monitoring.

Service provider responsibilities: service provider is to provide a complete proposal and budget as well as answer any questions and provide any additional information requested by the caseworker

Program area responsibilities: the risk assessment should be performed during the assessment phase of the process. The determined level of risk helps the caseworker establish the monitoring requirements for the contract.

Note: for additional information regarding the completion of the Risk Assessment, please refer to the Financial Monitoring Policy, 2. Risk Assessment.

Boards and governing boards

Working definition: a board may be an advisory board or a governing board, and it is important to clarify the differences.

Advisory boards are incorporated under a host organization and defines their limits of authority and responsibility. The advisory board has no legal status apart from its parent body. The primary function of an advisory board is to provide advice and recommendations to the agency's staff or the governing board, which may or may not take the advice it is given. The advisory board is only responsible for the mandate it receives from its parent body.

Governing boards are independently incorporated and are the legal entity and authority for the non-profit organization. In legal terms, the governing board is the organization. It has the authority to govern itself and to create its own rules. The governing board is ultimately accountable for the proper conduct of the organization's business and all aspects of the organization’s programs, staff and funds. This includes accountability to the funding source(s) of the agency.

Board Governance

The board’s primary responsibility and task is governance. Governance involves strategic direction and policy, and is made up of four interrelated responsibilities:

Establishing and implementing the organization’s mission and vision.

Setting the rate of progress the organization takes in accomplishing its mission and vision.

Providing continuity for the management and implementation of the organization’s affairs.

Securing community support for all aspects of the organization.

Governance, or directing, is different than managing. Managing focuses on the present. Governance focuses on the future. Governance leads the board to explore options and establish long-term goals and plans. Through good governance, the board delegates authority and responsibility to management and staff for day-to-day activities.

Governance creates goals and ensures that the proper organizational structure of management, systems and people is in place to achieve those goals. At the same time, governance requires that the structure maintains the organization’s integrity, reputation and responsibility to stakeholders.

Service provider responsibilities: service provider provides all bylaws, articles, terms of reference and any planning documents from the Board of Directors.

Caseworker responsibilities: prior to signing the contract, the caseworker asks the Board of Director’s representative to provide a copy of the resolution authorizing the organization to enter into the particular contract with Manitoba. The resolution authorizing the organization to enter into the contract should clearly say that it was passed by a majority of the board present at a duly convened meeting of the Board of Directors.

Note: caseworkers must ensure that a copy of the resolution is placed in the paper file.

  1. 3. Service provider overpayments

Working definition: overpayment is a debt owed to the Province of Manitoba by a service provider. Overpayment is a result of a service provider receiving funds in excess of what the service provider was entitled to and/or amounts deemed to be allowable expenditures in relation to the prescribed business activity of the project. It is important to determine if the service provider has an outstanding overpayment as it may affect whether or not the program area is able to contract with them.

Reasons for an overpayment:

Service provider may have been paid more money than the expenditures allowed in the contractual agreement.

Fraudulent activity or financial mismanagement within a program area contract.

Service provider responsibilities: service provider should disclose to the caseworker any/all overpayments they have with the province of Manitoba.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker should ask the service provider if they have any overpayments with the program area or any other government of Manitoba department. The caseworker should check the Integrated Case Management (ICM) system case notes to determine if there is an overpayment initiated in the system. If there is an outstanding overpayment with the service provider, the caseworker must find out the details of the overpayment.

Once it has been determined that an overpayment exists or has been established for a service provider, the following steps should be taken:

When an agreement is in progress and the final claim has not been processed:

Overpayment should be communicated to the service provider in writing.

A repayment of the funds by the service provider should be requested by the program area as soon as it is determined that an overpayment exists. If the service provider has the funds available, a cheque should be issued to Manitoba immediately.

If a monetary holdback exists, the amount of the overpayment would be deducted from the holdback, or the holdback would not be released.

If the service provider does not have the funds, the caseworker should determine what the funds were used for and why they are not available.

All information should be documented in ICM case notes and in the program area file.

When a renewal contract is under consideration:

Overpayment discovered prior to the signing of a renewal agreement

If the overpayment is discovered prior to the signing of a renewal contract then the overpayment is to be paid in full prior to the signing of the new agreement.

If the service provider is unable to make the payment in full caseworker should determine what the funds were used for and why they are not available. This must be documented in ICM case notes and in the program area file.

In exceptional circumstances where the service provider is unable to make the payment in full prior to the signing of the new agreement and the program area chooses to proceed with contracting specific steps must be taken.

An overpayment must be made:

Caseworker must ensure the service provider is not requesting additional funds to cover the overpayment.

The new agreement must contain a clause specifying that receipts are required with every claim form submission. Payment will not be released if all receipts for expenditures are not present.

The new agreement must contain a clause specifying the date when the overpayment must be paid in full.

Overpayment discovered after the signing of a renewal agreement and prior the

overpayment creation in SAP

If the service provider is unable to make the payment in full the caseworker should determine what the funds were used for and why they are not available.

The new agreement must be amended to contain a clause specifying that receipts are required with every claim form submission. Payment will not be released if all receipts for expenditures are not present.

The new agreement must be amended to contain a clause specifying the date when the overpayment must be paid in full.

The overpayment amount should not be recouped directly from the renewal contract. The service provider must provide a cheque to Manitoba.

If the overpayment is set up due to fraudulent activity, financial mismanagement, or it is with another government of Manitoba department, the decision to contract with the service provider should be discussed with the program area manager. If there still is a request to contract with the service provider, the manager or the program area director would contact Finance and Accountability at: WPG139 - TES Finance to discuss the possibility/consequences of contracting.

Funds from other sources

Working definition: funds from other sources are financial contributions from someone other than the service provider or the program area. The program area encourages the service provider to obtain funding from sources other than the program area to cover contracted activity costs.

Service provider responsibilities: the program area requires evidence that funding from other sources has been confirmed. The service provider must provide a letter of confirmation from the other funding source indicating when it will be provided, the amount to be provided, what is being provided for specifically, who has the authority to provide it and under what conditions the funding would be suspended. The service provider must show what costs the funds from other sources will cover and alternatives for funds should this source do not provide the funding.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker should review the letter of confirmation from the other funding source. The budget should be verified to see that the partners would not be receiving remuneration from the caseworker as a result of project funding. The caseworker must determine the viability of the project in case funding is not received from the other source. The agreement must include detail of the cost categories in which the contributions apply. Caseworkers must ensure that all letters regarding funding from other partners are placed in a file.

If the service provider is in receipt of contributions from any other level or government department, it must be confirmed that the service provider will not be reimbursed more than once for the same project costs.

If more funds than were anticipated were received from other sources, a provision must be included in the Schedule D for repayment to the program area where the excess amount or a decrease is to be made in the program area funding amount. If there is an increased level of activity due to additional funding from the other source, the program area is not obligated to reimburse for any increases to the costs stipulated in the agreement.

In-kind contributions

Working definition: in-kind contributions are contributions, other than cash, made by the service provider towards the contract costs. In-kind contributions are accepted in situations where the caseworker has determined they are required and relevant to the program area contract activities.

Service provider responsibilities: program area requires details about in-kind contributions from the applicant or any other sources and must include an explanation of how the values of the in-kind contributions were determined. They must also show that the contributions are required for the contracted activities.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker should request an independent assessment of the worth of any in-kind contribution that they feel might be questionable. Depending on the asset, independent assessments from different sources may be required. It is important to indicate in the agreement which cost categories the in-kind contributions apply to and their value. The applicant’s budget must be verified to see that the partner providing the in-kind contribution will not be receiving remuneration as a result of project funding. In-kind contributions can generally be considered if they are specific to the project, quantifiable, and are essential to the project. These could include time, resources or knowledge that can be detailed and justified, physical assets, or space that the project would have otherwise rented. Caseworker should discuss any concerns with their program area manager.

Profit generation

Working definition: any surplus balance existing after actual approved expenditures are subtracted from all revenue is considered profit. Program area funded activities should not generate profit. All expenditures must be verified to ensure the program area is only reimbursing approved actual costs.

Service provider responsibilities: when submitting the proposal, the service provider must be stating the estimated actual costs. Any profit generated must be used to offset the program area’s contribution. The profit should be shown on the claim form and deducted from the claims’ total.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker will reimburse the service provider for actual costs. This should make it difficult for them to realize a profit.

Revenue generation

Working definition: revenue is the gross income from the sales of goods and services, prior to the deduction of cost of goods, services and expenses. Revenue may include funds from other sources, donations, in-kind contributions or interest earned on money in the bank. All revenue that a service provider generates on the program area contractual agreement requires an equivalent decline in payments made to the service provider.

Service provider responsibilities: any revenue generated must be used to offset the program area’s contribution. The revenue should be shown on the claim form and deducted from the total.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker must review all instances of revenue generation. There are instances where projects generate revenue through project activities. All revenue should initiate a downward amendment of contract funds available to the service provider. Revenue should be identified during the assessment stage, but caseworkers have to monitor as this can change during the life of the contract.

Visual identity guidelines

Working definition: all forms of public communication including promotion, advertising, printed materials and publicity must adhere to the Visual Identity Guidelines established by the Province of Manitoba. All forms of public communication must be reviewed and approved by Communications and Engagement Division (CED).

Service provider responsibilities: the program area requires third party service providers to adhere to the Visual Identity Guidelines established by the province of Manitoba. All forms of public communication must be reviewed and approved by the program area and CED.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker should provide the service provider the Public Communication Guidelines for Service Providers. The program area can pay for communication costs pre-approved by the caseworker.

First Nations bands

Working definition: Indian and Northern Affairs Canada states, a band is a body of Aboriginal people for whose collective use and benefit lands have been set apart or money is held by the Crown or declared to be a band for the purposes of the Indian Act. Each band has its own governing band council, usually consisting of one Chief and several councillors. Community members choose the chief and councillors by election, or sometimes through custom. The members of a band generally share common values, traditions and practices rooted in their ancestral heritage. Today, many bands prefer to be known as First Nations (e.g., the Batchewana Band is now called the Batchewana First Nation).

A. Band council This is the governing body for a band. It usually consists of a Chief and councillors, who are elected for two or three-year terms (under the Indian Act or band custom) to carry out band business, which may include education; water, sewer and fire services; by-laws; community buildings; schools; roads; and other community businesses and services.”

Service provider responsibilities: for a contract to be properly authorized by a band so as to bind on the band, the band must have approved the contract in the following manner:

Band council must have discussed the contract at a duly convened band council meeting.

Resolution to approve the contract must have been presented or read by the mover and duly moved and seconded, must have been placed before the meeting by the presiding officer and opened for discussion.

Discussion at the meeting must have been followed by a Band Council Resolution (BCR) that was signed by a majority of the band councillors present at the meeting.

Caseworker responsibilities: prior to signing the contract, the caseworker will ask the band’s representative to provide a copy of the BCR authorizing the band to enter into the particular contract with Manitoba. The BCR authorizing the band to enter into the contract should clearly say that it was passed by a majority of the band councillors present at a duly convened meeting of the band council.

Note: all known projects can be listed in one BCR. Any new projects will require a BCR.

B. Co-management: third party managers appointed by Indigenous and Northern Affairs Canada (INAC) are not authorized to deal with any funding for a band besides INAC funding therefore they do not have the capacity to contract on behalf. This third party has no bearing on a band’s authority to enter into a contract with the program area.

Caseworker responsibilities: in entering into a contract with a band for which INAC has appointed a third party manager, the program area may be taking risks that it would not usually take with other contracts. Contracts should be carefully drafted to ensure that they contain provisions that fully protect the program area’s interests. The contract should provide safeguards to ensure that the funding is used only for the purposes intended by the program and no other purposes. It is very important before entering into a contract that the caseworker verify:

Third party manager has not been authorized by the band to enter into contracts on its behalf.

Band Chief and council have properly authorized the band to enter into the contract with the program area.

Proposed signatory for the band is either the Chief and council or someone who has been authorized by the Chief and council, by BCR, to sign the contract.

Contract contains sufficient safeguards to fully protect the program area’s interests.

Note: Caseworker must ensure that a copy of the BCR is placed in the paper file.

  1. 11. Service provider tax implications (receiving program area funding)

Working definition: funds received from a contribution agreement are considered nontaxable for the receiving organization.

Service provider responsibilities: service provider is responsible to file income tax returns as per Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) requirements.

Caseworker responsibilities: none.

Goods and Services Tax (GST)

Working definition: GST is a five percent tax on the consumption of goods and services.

Contractual Services

The Reciprocal Taxation Agreement between the Province of Manitoba and the Government of Canada specifies that when purchases are made for goods or services consumed by the province, the invoice is sent directly to the province, and the province pays the supplier directly, that purchase is exempt from GST. Therefore, the program area will not pay GST on services provided in the contractual agreement.

The exemption should be communicated to the service provider by providing them with the Exemption Statement Letter.

Note: Exemption Statement letter is not to be used by the service provider in purchasing services or supplies. The letter strictly states that the program area is exempt from paying GST on direct purchase.

Third party purchases

This exemption does not apply to third party purchases. A third party purchase includes those instances where a service provider pays for the supply/service and is then reimbursed by the program area. The program area reimburses a service provider for the amount of GST that it pays less any rebate or credit the service provider may receive from CRA.

The contract should indicate that the program area is GST exempt and that GST is a reimbursable expenditure minus any entitled tax credit/rebate to be received from CRA. Caseworkers should ask the service provider if it receives an exemption or rebate for GST.

It is the responsibility of the service provider to contact CRA and to obtain any potential GST rebates that it is entitled to.

Section B: project staff costs and other guidelines

These guidelines ensure service providers have clear information regarding costs associated with project staff.

Each employee should be made aware of these guidelines when hired. It is not the program area’s role or responsibility to develop service provider personnel guidelines. If there is a collective (union) agreement in place, then that agreement would apply. The caseworker must ensure that the service provider has all of the various personnel policies/guidelines documented. The program area must receive a copy of the personnel policies/guidelines or the collective agreement for the project file.

  1. 1. Mandatory employment related costs (MERC’s)

Working definition: MERC’s are the basic salary-related costs that an employer must deduct from the amount paid to their staff and forward to CRA. The amounts are updated annually and include Employment Insurance (EI) deduction, Canada Pension Plan (CPP) contributions, vacation pay (when time is not taken) and the Manitoba Health and Education Levy.

Service provider responsibilities: service provider must make the appropriate deductions from the employees’ salary and submit the correct remittance amount to CRA on a monthly basis.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker must inform the service provider that the program area will only fund MERC’s related to employees that are part of the contracted services. The program area will not pay late remittance penalties the service provider may incur.

  1. 2. Vacation leave or pay

Working definition: all employees covered under Manitoba’s Employment Standards Code are entitled to annual vacations with pay. For each year worked for the same employer, an employee is entitled to a two week paid vacation. After five consecutive years, this rises to three weeks of paid annual vacation.

Service provider responsibilities: personnel guidelines are the responsibility of the service provider. Each employee should be made aware of the vacation pay guidelines when hired.

Caseworker responsibilities: salaried staff who have been continuously employed for more than 52 weeks on a project are entitled under legislation to be paid vacation leave. Such vacation leave should be identified at the negotiation stage and to avoid contributing to vacation leave twice, it should not be included as wages subject to the program area’s contribution.

Vacation pay is calculated at 4%, unless the employee has completed five years or more of service, at which point it is 6%. The program area pays for vacation pay in situations where the individual has not taken time off. If time off is taken, the employee will receive a regular cheque, but the program area does not contribute the 4% or 6 % for this period.

  1. 4. Jury duty

Working definition: the Jury Act of Manitoba states that every employer must grant to an employee who is summoned to serve as a juror a leave of absence from their employment. The leave of absence can be with or without pay. When the employee returns to work following jury duty, the employer must reinstate the employee to his or her position or a similar position with the same salary. An employer who fails to comply with this provision of The Jury Act is liable to the employee for any loss as a result of that failure. It is an offence for an employer to directly or indirectly threaten to cause or cause an employee loss of position or employment or threatens to impose or impose on an employee any monetary or other penalty because of the employee being summoned for jury service. After ten days of serving as a juror on a trial, jurors are paid a jury fee for the remaining days of the trial.

Service provider responsibilities: it is the responsibility of the service provider to decide whether or not to pay the employee unless jury duty has been negotiated through collective (union) agreements. The decision is shared with caseworker. The service provider will advise if the absence has any impact on the services provided to their participants.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker must ensure that the services provided to

participants continue as agreed.

  1. 7. Overtime for project staff

Working definition: Manitoba Employment Standards Code states, “All hours worked beyond the standard hours of work must be paid at a rate of 1.5 times the employee’s regular rate of pay. Overtime can only be worked with the employer’s permission or knowledge and/or at the employer’s request. An employer and an employee may agree in writing that the employee takes paid time off during his or her regular hours instead of overtime pay. The paid time off must be a minimum of 1.5 times the number of hours of overtime worked. The regular wage rate is paid for the time off.”

Service provider responsibilities: service provider must make salaried staff aware of the personnel guidelines or collective agreement. Overtime can only be worked with the Service provider’s permission for knowledge and/or at the service provider’s request.

Caseworker responsibilities: caseworker is not responsible to pay for overtime.

Complaints from participants received by the caseworker/program area

Working Definition: participants complain about project staff for a variety of reasons. The complaint may be of a personal nature or perhaps related to the project activities.

(Not related to negotiated financial assistance)

It is the program area’s responsibility to ensure that the participant is receiving the services as per the agreement.

Introduction

The Workers Compensation Act/Workers Compensation Board of Manitoba

In Canada, workers compensation is a system of compulsory no-fault insurance for workplace injuries. The system is administered by statutory corporations or commissions established under the legislation of each province or territory. The Manitoba legislation is called The Workers Compensation Act (the WCA).

The workers compensation system (administered through the Workers Compensation Board of Manitoba or WCB) is an accident insurance system for employers and workers for determining compensation for workplace injuries. The WCB has statutory responsibility for all matters concerning the workers compensation system in Manitoba including adjudication and administrative enforcement of workers compensation matters.

The WCB of Manitoba pays compensation to injured workers covered under the WCA. Currently, the WCA covers approximately 70 percent of Manitoba’s workers.

Coverage is compulsory for industries named in the WCA - referred to as compulsory’ industries or employers.

Non-covered employers may apply for voluntary coverage - referred to as non-compulsory or voluntary industries or employers.

There are also several explicit exclusions.

Employers who pay into the system are not liable for the workplace injuries or diseases sustained by their workers. In turn, workers injured in the course of employment are automatically entitled to compensation regardless of fault. However, the workers give up their right of legal action against a potentially negligent employer in return for the certainty of no-fault benefits. With no-fault compensation, benefits are paid to injured workers whether or not negligence on the part of the worker or employer contributed to the accident. The injured worker may be eligible for compensation for the duration on the injury’s impact on the worker’s employment. Assessments are levied upon employers and gathered into a common fund out of which benefits are paid to workers who are injured as a result of their employment.

Employer Classifications

In Manitoba, the WCB has two broad classifications of employers:

a) Compulsory (mandatory) industries or employers

There is no requirement for application or admission.

Coverage is automatic, whether or not assessments have been paid.

Even if a covered employer has not paid assessments to the WCB, injured workers are covered under the Workers Compensation Act (WCA) and are entitled to compensation from the WCB. Similarly, whether or not the employer has paid assessments, workers in industries covered by the WCA do not have the right to sue their employers. The Construction and Food Services Industries are examples of mandatory employers.

Note:

The WCB has exclusive jurisdiction to determine whether a person is a worker within a covered industry. Worker has a very broad definition but an employer-employee relationship must exist and the individual must be employed for more than 24 hours a week by the same employer.

Contracted service providers - if the service provider is in the compulsory workers compensation category under the Workers Compensation Act of Manitoba, they must ensure that each participant and staff member is covered by workers’s compensation and provide satisfactory evidence that the required coverage is in place upon request.

b) Non-compulsory (non-mandatory) or voluntary industries or employers

Employers in non-compulsory industries may apply for coverage. Certain persons who would not otherwise be workers may receive voluntary coverage, including directors of a corporation, and certain resident family members of a director or employer.

Independent contractors, who are self-employed persons, engaged in a compulsory industry, other than deemed workers.

Farm labourers, on application by the employer.

Employers or individuals with voluntary coverage are treated the same as employers in compulsory industries. Other non-compulsory industries include insurance, real estate, financial institutions, education, and independent contractors who are self-employed in a non-compulsory industry. A florist shop is an example of a non-mandatory employer.

Contracted service providers - caseworkers direct contracted service providers who are classified as a non-mandatory employer to either obtain coverage through workers compensation or obtain coverage through a third party.

WCB determines who is responsible for paying for claims to injured workers (program area’s insured participants) based on WCB internal guidelines.

The organization that produces the T4 or T5 for a participant is generally responsible for the WCB coverage and/or paying benefits in case of an injury.

The organization that refers the participant to authorized training is generally responsible for the WCB coverage and/or paying benefits in case of an injury. Authorized training refers to all courses approved by the program area.

In which a worker is killed;

In which a worker suffers:

Asset classes and depreciation

Different asset classes depreciate at different rates. The Straight-Line Method is used to calculate the depreciation and net book value required for the disposal of assets. The assets cannot be given a value other than the amount determined through this calculation process.

Asset Class Estimated Useful Life

Computer Hardware 36 months

Machinery/Equipment 60 months

Furniture 60 months

Formula:

Monthly depreciation charges = cost of asset divided by the estimated useful life

Total depreciation charge for the asset = monthly depreciation charges multiplied by the number of months asset is used

Net book value = original cost of the asset less the total depreciation charge for the asset. This is now the value the item to be sold.

Example:

Computer cost $3250.00. It was purchased September 1, 2005. It will be sold August 31, 2007.

Depreciation: $3250.00 X 24 months divided by 36 months = $2166.66

Net Book Value = $3250 - 2166.66 = $1,083.34

Section I - Conflict of Interest

The Province of Manitoba is charged with the responsibility of protecting the public interest, particularly about accountability for the spending of tax dollars. External agencies are perceived by the public as extensions of government. As such, Boards of Directors and/or employees of external agencies delivering services on behalf of government are accountable to the public and are particularly vulnerable to charges of conflict of interest.

As a funder of many external agencies, the program area is requesting that agencies share their conflict of interest policy and guidelines. The caseworker requests a copy of their documentation. If, agencies have not developed their own conflict of interest policy and guidelines, then they should be directed to review this guideline to develop guidelines for their Board of Directors and/or employees. Many organizations that the program area funds do not have a Board of Directors; however, it is recommended that this guideline is communicated to the service providers. These are guidelines only. It is recommended that caseworkers discuss specific issues with the program area manager.

By stating clearly, the standards of conduct expected of board members and/or employees, the guidelines serve as a preventative measure, so board members and/or employees do not inadvertently place themselves in a position of perceived, potential, or actual conflict of interest. Furthermore, the sections dealing with disclosure and appeals provide for avenues to clarify and resolve issues before they become a problem.

The aim of conflict of interest guidelines is to strike a balance between legitimate protection of public interest and the protection of the board members' and/or employees' personal and professional interests.

It is the responsibility of the Board of Directors or employees to ensure that their guidelines are communicated to all board members and/or employees of their organization. It is also their responsibility to establish procedures for ensuring compliance with the standards set out in their organization’s conflict of interest policy.

Note: if the program area is contributing most of the funding (i.e. 75% or more) for the project activities, then the program area expects a conflict of interest policy/guidelines to be developed by the organization.

Conflict of interest policy statement

The expects Boards of Directors and employees of external agencies which it funds, to maintain high standards of integrity, impartiality, and ethical conduct. Board members and employees must be constantly aware of the need to avoid situations which might result either in actual, potential, or perceived misconduct or conflicts of interest and to conduct themselves in a manner which commands the respect and confidence of their fellow citizens.

A conflict of interest policy, including disclosure requirements, applies to all members of the Boards of Directors and all employees of external agencies. These suggested guidelines should complement rather than replace the provisions of relevant legislation, or any other statute, collective agreement, rule or statement which applies to Boards of Directors or employees of external agencies, and in the event of a conflict, relevant legislation shall govern and supersede these guidelines.

Definition

A conflict of interest is any situation in which a board member or employee of an external agency has an employment, business or personal interest which results or appears to result in:

an improper material interest or an advantage by virtue of the person's position

an interference with the objective exercise of the person's duties

A material interest includes any matter or situation where a board member or employee has a direct or indirect financial or other interest beyond the interest of an ordinary citizen.

Requirements for disclosure

Members of the Boards of Directors and employees of external agencies are responsible for disclosure of any situation or matter where they have an actual or perceived conflict of interest or the potential for a conflict of interest.

Conflict of interest declarations should be filed to the Board Chair and if necessary shared with the program area annually at a minimum or updated immediately where:

an actual, potential, or perceived conflict situation arises where none existed previously.

change occurs which alters the nature or degree of the conflict, after a declaration was made.

Where a conflict of interest has been found to exist, the board member or employee, if necessary, will be required to take steps to avoid the conflict of interest. Where a perceived or potential conflict situation may exist, the board member or employee will be provided with advice on what steps need to be taken to remove the perception of or the potential for a conflict of interest.

Scenario one

Where a board member is unsure whether any conflict of interest may exist, it is the

board member’s responsibility to seek clarification from the Board of Directors.

A board member shall disclose in writing to the Board of Directors, or request to have entered in the minutes of meetings of the Board of Directors, the nature and extent of his/her interest.

The Board of Directors shall decide by majority vote of other members at the meeting whether a perceived or actual conflict of interest exists in the case of a board member.

No board member shall be present during any discussions of the board or vote on any matter where it has been decided that a material interest exists. The minutes of the board meeting shall in each case record the member's disclosure of interest and the fact that they took no part in the discussion or decision. In addition, the board member must refrain from attempting, directly or indirectly, to influence the decision of the board.

The Board of Directors may choose to delegate to an executive committee of the board, the authority to decide whether a material interest exists for board members.

If the executive committee of the board cannot decide, the matter shall be referred to the full board for decision.

Scenario two

Where an employee is unsure whether any conflict of interest may exist, it is their responsibility to seek clarification from the Board of Directors or the board's delegate.

An agency employee shall disclose in writing to the Board of Directors, or the Board's delegate, the nature and extent of their interest.

The Board of Directors shall decide by majority vote whether a perceived, potential, or actual conflict of interest exists in the case of an employee.

The Board of Directors may choose to delegate to the executive director or executive management committee, the authority to decide whether a material interest exists for employees, subject to a quarterly review and ratification of those decisions by the board.

If the Board's delegate cannot decide, the matter shall be referred to the Board of Directors for decision.

No employee shall participate in negotiations, decision making or activities where it has been decided that a material interest exists.

Guidelines

The range, complexity, and unique nature of individual external agencies' activities are such that it is not possible to outline all conflict-of-interest situations.

Board members and employees shall not engage directly or indirectly in any personal business transaction or private arrangement for personal profit which accrues from or is based upon their official position or authority or upon confidential or non-public information which they gain by reason of such position or authority.

Board members and employees shall not divulge confidential or restricted information to any unauthorized person or release such information in advance of authorization for its release.

Board members and employees shall not act in any official matter where there is a personal interest which is incompatible with an unbiased exercise of official judgement.

Board members and employees must declare where they have direct or indirect personal business or financial activities which conflict with their official duties and responsibilities.

Board members and employees shall not place themselves in a position where they are under obligation to any persons who might benefit from special considerations or favours on their part.

  1. 5. Appeal mechanisms

A board member (if a board is in place) who disputes the manner of application of the agency’s conflict of interest guidelines within their agency may normally appeal such application to an independent arbitrator agreed to by both parties.

An employee who disputes the manner of application of conflict of interest guidelines may appeal such application to the Board of Directors (if in place) or an appropriate signatory of the agency/business (i.e. owner, coordinator, manager).

Support to participants who participate on service provider contracted project work experience or on-the-job activities fall under three categories:

Unpaid work experience - no direct program area financial support for living allowances and no wage directly from the employer (some participants may remain on Employment and Income Assistance (EIA).

Paid work experience - participants receive direct financial support from the program area in the form of a living allowance (EI Part I or Part II or a living allowance paid from the program area appropriation) but no wage directly from the employer.

Employer paid work experience - participants receive a wage directly from the employer providing the work experience.

This policy applies to all three types of work experience defined above and provided to participants through contracted service providers.

Note: this policy does not apply to participants participating in work experience/on-the-job training through the wage subsidy programs.

  1. 2. Labour and Immigration, Employment Standards division

Except as otherwise authorized under the Employment Standards Code, an employer shall not pay an employee less than the prescribed minimum wage. However, Minimum Wages and Working Conditions Regulation 62/99 of The Employment Standards Code has two types of exclusions for training/work experience that are pertinent to program area contracted service provider programming (those who provide services to clients related to on-the-job training).

The first exclusion is as follows:

2 -  Code does not apply to an employee who: (c) is given training or work experience for a limited period of time through a program implemented or approved by a provincial or federal government authority, or, a school board, as defined under The Public Schools Act, and is not paid a wage.

This exclusion is a complete exclusion from all aspects of the code. The way Employment Standards interprets this provision is that there is no employment relationship with the employer.  Since it is time limited and uses the words work experience, the sense is that the employer would not be truly benefiting from the employee being at work. It is more of a public service to help an individual gain valuable experience. The time limited aspect of this provision is determined on a case by case basis. The question to be asked is: how long did take for the individual to gain the work experience or to be trained in what they were doing.

The key to this provision is implemented or approved by a provincial authority. 

The other exclusion is:  

12  -  Section 11 (Minimum Wage) does not apply to employees who are employed under a training scheme implemented or approved by: (a) provincial or federal government authority; or (b) jointly by provincial and federal government authorities and who are paid a wage.

This exclusion is slightly different in that it recognizes an employment relationship with an employer, is not time limited, and only allows the payment of a wage below the minimum wage.  All other aspects of the code would apply such as vacation pay, etc.

Labour and Immigration stated that these would be the back to work programs for social assistance recipients, retraining programs for Employment Insurance (EI) recipients, etc.

The above two exclusions are sufficient to allow program area participants to participate in work experience or on-the-job training contracted project activities and not receive a wage. The program area and the contracted service providers/employers are not in violation of the Employment Standards Code when program area participants participate in unpaid work experience/on-the-job training.

However, in the interests of program area participants and to ensure that service providers and employers providing work experience to program area participants are accountable for the duration and nature of those activities, the program area

requires the service provider to draft a MOU between their organization and the work experience employer/organization. This MOU outlines the agreed upon details of the clients work experience activity.

  1. 3. Memorandum of Understanding between the prgram area contracted service provider and work experience employer/organization

Work experience placements must be agreed upon by the contracted service provider (on behalf of the project participants) and the employer/organization (and the union if applicable) where the participant is to be placed.

Note: before undertaking work experience placements, the program area and the service provider must ensure that either the employer/organization or the contracted service provider has a workplace insurance policy which will cover their work experience placement participants.

For addition information, please refer to Section D – Workers Compensation/Third Party Liability Insurance for Project Staff and Participants.

The program area outlines the requirement for service providers to draft a MOU between the service provider and work experience employer/organization. This MOU outlines the agreed upon details of the clients work experience activity.

The program area provides the details of the MOU requirements to their contracted service providers who fall under this policy. Service providers have the responsibility to draft the documents.

MOU details should include at minimum (this is not an all-inclusive list):

Service provider information: organization name, address, contact name and phone number.

Employer name, address, contact name and phone number.

Client name(s).

Start and end dates of work experience and hours per day/week.

Supervision and monitoring - who will provide and timeframe(s).

Job description/duties/activities and skills to be learned.

Training/coaching - who will provide and timeframe(s).

Reference that the project activities are being funded by the program area.

Reference that the participant does not receive reimbursement while participating on the work experience.

Signatures and dates.

Responsibility for workers compensation coverage or third party liability insurance coverage.

The caseworker outlines the MOU requirements in the agreement schedule B or D. This directs the service provider to develop and use the MOU with their work experience employers outlining the information that is required (example above).
